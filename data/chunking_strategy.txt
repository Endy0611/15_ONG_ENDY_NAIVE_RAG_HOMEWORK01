Chunking Strategies in RAG

Chunking is the step where a full document is broken down into smaller pieces before those pieces are embedded and stored. The way a document is chunked has a big effect on retrieval quality, because a chunk that is too long or too short changes what the embedding model actually captures.

The simplest approach is fixed-size chunking: cut the text every N characters or tokens, regardless of sentence or paragraph boundaries. This is easy to implement and works on any kind of text, even messy documents with no clear structure. Its main weakness is that it can cut a sentence or an idea in half right at the chunk boundary. To reduce that problem, fixed-size chunking is usually combined with an overlap, meaning each new chunk repeats the last portion of the previous chunk so that context near a boundary is not completely lost.

A more advanced approach is recursive or structure-aware chunking, which tries to split on natural boundaries first, such as paragraphs, then sentences, and only falls back to a hard character cut if a single paragraph is still too long. This tends to keep related ideas together better than pure fixed-size splitting, at the cost of slightly more complex code.

Semantic chunking goes a step further by using embeddings themselves to decide where to split: sentences are grouped together as long as they remain semantically similar, and a new chunk starts once the topic shifts enough. This produces chunks that are more coherent as standalone pieces of context, but it is more expensive to compute since it requires embedding at a finer granularity before the final chunks are even formed.

There is no single correct chunk size for every use case. Short, dense documents like FAQs may work well with smaller chunks, since each answer is usually self-contained. Longer narrative documents may need larger chunks with more overlap so that a single retrieved chunk still makes sense on its own once it is pulled out of its original context and handed to the language model.
